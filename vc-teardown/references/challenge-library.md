# Challenge Library

Standard attack surfaces. Work through them; skip the ones that don't apply. Each entry gives the challenge, what evidence to look for, and the shape of the plan change it usually forces.

## 1. Competition — "this is already built"

The most common fatal flaw and the first thing to check. Search before assuming a gap.

Look for: named incumbents, their funding and traffic, app store ranking, governing-body or platform endorsement, and whether they're well-capitalized or thin.

Calibration matters. An incumbent with $3M raised and a million monthly users is beatable *in a segment they don't serve* but unbeatable head-on at their core feature. An incumbent with $200M is a different conversation. Say which.

**Typical change:** demote the contested feature to an acquisition asset; move the value proposition to an adjacent unserved segment.

## 2. Free substitutes — "what do people use today?"

Most ideas die against Google Maps, a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, or a paper form — not against a funded startup. The workaround people currently tolerate defines your real bar.

A visible, ugly workaround is a gift: it proves the pain is real *and* that nobody has served it. Quote it.

**Typical change:** position against the workaround explicitly, and make the switching cost from it near zero.

## 3. Monetization — "does this revenue line actually exist?"

Interrogate each proposed line against real rates:

- **Advertising** needs enormous traffic. Niche content monetizes at low single-digit RPM — 100k monthly sessions is a few hundred dollars. Almost never a startup revenue line. Sponsorship of a specific event or league is real money; display is not.
- **Marketplace take rate** requires GMV that requires liquidity you don't have on day one. Model when it turns on, not that it exists.
- **Consumer subscription** is the hardest sell in software, especially against a free incumbent.
- **SMB SaaS** is the most reliable line for small teams: real budget, short cycles, low churn when it's operational.
- **Data and insights** is often the sleeper — if the product generates information nobody else has, someone with a budget usually needs it.

**Typical change:** reorder revenue lines by when they can realistically turn on; cut the ones that need scale the plan won't reach.

## 4. Willingness to pay — "who signs, and what's their alternative?"

Name the person who signs. If nobody has a budget line for this, that's the finding.

Best available evidence: reviews of competitors' paid tiers. Users state the objection directly and it's usually the same objection your product will face.

Ask whether the payer and the user are the same person. When they aren't, the plan needs two motions.

**Typical change:** move monetization from the user to the institution; or find the venue/employer/organizer who already has budget for the manual version.

## 5. Cold start — "what does the first user see?"

Marketplaces and social products die empty. The specific question: what does user #1 experience on day one in a city with no supply?

Nationwide launch is the classic error — it means every city starts empty and every early user churns permanently. You get one chance with each person.

**Typical change:** one metro, one venue, one community. National ambition, hyperlocal execution. Do not open registration outside the beachhead until liquidity is proven.

## 6. Distribution — "how does anyone find out?"

Rank channels by honest expected value, not by what's easy to describe. SEO against an entrenched incumbent is usually a loss; paid acquisition usually exceeds LTV early.

The strongest channels are almost always *granted* rather than *won*: an institution tells its users to adopt the thing. Look hard for who could do that.

**Typical change:** find the entity with existing authority over the users and make them the channel.

## 7. Buyer incentive — "why would they bother?"

Applies to any supply-side or B2B2C hook. "Claim your listing" works when the platform already sends leads, and not before.

Reframe the ask from marketing exposure (which requires traffic you lack) to operational relief (which requires nothing). The question is always: what is annoying this person *today*?

**Typical change:** rewrite the pitch from "get discovered" to "stop doing this manually."

## 8. Sales cycle — "how long until money?"

Enterprise and municipal buyers can take 6–18 months with procurement and RFPs, which kills a bootstrapped plan regardless of product quality.

Two useful moves: price under the discretionary threshold so an individual manager can sign without procurement; and find the person inside the organization who feels the pain but has no budget — they become the champion who creates the budget conversation.

**Typical change:** parallel motion into a faster-buying segment (SMB, HOA, franchise, independent operator) while the slow one matures.

## 9. Data availability — "does this data exist?"

Products that aggregate information frequently assume it exists somewhere. Check. Often it doesn't exist anywhere, in any form.

This looks fatal and usually isn't. Non-existent data is the strongest moat available — whoever generates it owns it, and it can't be scraped, licensed, or copied. See `moat-patterns.md`.

**Typical change:** stop aggregating, start generating. The product becomes the instrument that creates the data.

## 10. Regulatory and liability — "what goes wrong?"

Underestimated in almost every consumer plan. Check for: strangers meeting in person, minors, health and safety, payments and money transmission, licensed professions, alcohol, housing and lending discrimination, accessibility (ADA), data protection, and local political disputes the product will be dragged into.

**Typical change:** terms and waiver, insurance quoted before launch, moderation and reporting from day one, explicit policy on what personal data is never published.

## 11. Founder-market fit — "why this person?"

Name the transferable assets honestly, and name the gap just as honestly. The gap is usually one of: distribution in the target community, domain credibility, or willingness to do unglamorous on-the-ground work.

This challenge frequently produces the most important plan change, because the answer is often "this plan requires a person who does not currently exist on the team."

**Typical change:** make recruiting the missing operator an explicit precondition rather than an implicit assumption.

## 12. Capital efficiency — "what does this cost to prove?"

Separate the cost of building from the cost of *learning*. They're usually different by an order of magnitude, and founders reliably spend on the first to avoid the second.

**Typical change:** replace the MVP with a thin slice or a manual test that answers the same question in weeks.

## 13. Defensibility — "what happens when it works?"

If a well-resourced competitor could clone this in a quarter, what stops them? Acceptable answers: proprietary data that accrues from usage, institutional switching cost, local network density, regulatory position, or a structural disincentive in the incumbent's business model.

"We'll execute better" and "first-mover advantage" are not answers.

**Typical change:** shift the plan toward whichever of the real answers is available.

## 14. Feature-not-a-company

The hardest thing to tell a founder. If the whole idea could be a feature inside an existing product, the plan needs either a wedge the incumbent won't take (metric mismatch, segment they won't serve, business model conflict) or an honest reframe as a feature to be sold or licensed.

Say it in those words when it's true. Softening it wastes their year.

## 15. Retention — "why does anyone come back?"

Discovery products get used once. Name the weekly recurring reason to open the thing. If it's a one-time transaction, the economics have to work on a single purchase, and the plan should say so.

**Typical change:** add a recurring loop — scheduling, notifications, streaks, status changes, or an obligation to other people.

## 16. Scale honesty

Compute the realistic revenue of one fully-penetrated beachhead. Multiply by the number of beachheads reachable in five years. If the result is a good $2M business rather than a $200M one, that is a finding, not a failure — but it must be stated, because pitching it wrongly fails diligence and misallocates the founder's next year.

**Typical change:** reframe the funding strategy — bootstrap, revenue-financed, or small seed — rather than reframing the business.
